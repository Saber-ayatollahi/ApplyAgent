## Likely questions (with model answers from the repo)

**1. "Walk me through a risk event you identified, escalated, and saw remediated."**
Use Story 2 verbatim: a client-delivery run produced portfolio sensitivities that passed every internal check but did not square with the client's economic intuition under a specific rate shock. I held the release rather than sign off under deadline pressure, decomposed the sensitivities by asset class, and isolated a curve-calibration edge case in short-end inversion handling. Escalated to product owners and the client's Head of Risk with a remediation plan; release slipped 48 hours, the defect was fixed upstream, and the case became a permanent validation test. I became that Head of Risk's direct escalation contact.

**2. "You have not run a COSO-based control testing team. How would you take that on?"**
Be direct: my control experience is model and process governance, not COSO-mapped operational control testing. The transferable mechanics are identical — control objective, control design, evidence of operating effectiveness, defect logging, escalation threshold, remediation tracking, re-test. Story 6 is the proof: I took a spreadsheet workflow with no logging or versioning, rebuilt it as a controlled Python pipeline, ran it in shadow mode for two reconciliation cycles, and closed the governance audit. My first 90 days would be spent with the existing testing team learning the card process taxonomy before I change anything.

**3. "How do you monitor regulatory change and turn it into process change?"**
EY IFRS 17/IFRS 9 (Story 5): a Canadian insurer had finance, actuarial, and IT out of sync on data sourcing and CSM mechanics. I built shared requirements documentation, walked each function through the others' constraints, isolated the three decisions that genuinely needed executive sign-off, and escalated those cleanly. The program hit its readiness milestone and the documentation was reused on later engagements. The pattern — read the requirement, map it to owners and processes, surface only the real decisions upward — is the same at a branch level.

**4. "How do you hold a line with a business partner who does not like your finding?"**
Same two stories, different framing. In the release-hold case the pressure was commercial and the deadline was real; I did not argue the conclusion, I decomposed the number until the disagreement became a fact question rather than an opinion question. Then I brought the remediation plan with the finding — never the finding alone. That is what converted an escalation into a trusted relationship.

**5. "Your background is quantitative. Why a first-line risk office?"**
Story 8 plus scope framing: chemical engineering gave the mathematics, the MSc in Financial Modelling translated it, and Ortec/EY/Moody's built the practitioner bench. What I have actually done for seven years is decide whether analytical output is defensible enough to release — that is risk ownership, not modelling. The Canada Risk Office is the same judgment applied to business processes rather than to numbers, at wider scope. Also use Story 9 to pre-empt the title challenge: sign-off at Moody's is delegated by role within a formal governance framework, and it attests to specific outputs, not to whole portfolios.

## The one competency gap to prepare for

**People leadership at scale.** The JD asks for a leader of a standing team; the honest record is an IC-plus-senior-review role in a ~12-person Modelling Services team, mentorship of juniors, a three-person UPP study team, and cross-functional program coordination at EY. Prepare a crisp answer: what I have led is work and decisions across functions, and the standing-team leap is the deliberate next step — with a concrete 30/60/90 for inheriting the control testing team (listen, map, keep cadence, then change one thing). Do not overstate this in the resume or the letter; own it verbally.
